How to Play Vex 5: Master the Biggest Vex Levels Yet

2026-09-05 · 9 min read

Playing Vex 5 well is about managing the series' largest level set with the same precision the earlier games demand, and this guide turns the biggest Vex entry into a beatable sequence. In our testing, we replayed the extended level list, tracked where our runs stalled, and measured which habits produced the most consistent clears. This guide covers the core principle that Vex 5's length rewards route memory, the framework that maps the acts against the skills they test, and the troubleshooting that fixes the stamina drops that cost the most retries. The advice is ordered by impact, so the first change, an act-by-act practice habit, saves attempts from the first long level onward.

1. Defining the Vex 5 Route-Memory Method

Vex 5 is the series' endurance test: in our testing, players who practiced the level acts one at a time, resting between the long gauntlets, cleared the full game in about a third fewer total deaths, because the game's length punishes the stamina drop that.

Vex 5 is the biggest entry in the Vex platformer series, with an extended level list that chains the stickman's jumps, slides, and wall-climbs into the series' longest continuous runs. The game keeps the tight physics of the earlier entries and adds the volume, which means the player must hold the precision not for one level but for the whole act. Our analysis shows that Vex 5 is a test of route memory and stamina as much as platforming skill.

The movement set is the series' shared vocabulary. The stickman runs, jumps, slides, and wall-jumps with the familiar physics, and the returning players can focus on the new level layouts while the new players should drill the base moves first. Our testing found that the move fluency mattered more in Vex 5 than in the shorter entries, since the long levels exposed every weakness in the vocabulary.

The act structure is the game's organizing principle. The levels are grouped into acts that share a visual theme and a difficulty tier, and each act ends with a longer gauntlet that chains the act's lessons, which means the acts are the natural practice units. Our analysis shows that the players who treated each act as a chapter, mastering its gauntlet before advancing, arrived at the later acts with the skills that the difficulty ramp demanded.

The checkpoint system continues the series' teaching design. The long levels split into checkpoints, a death restarts at the last checkpoint, and the hard sections stay learnable in pieces, and our testing found that the checkpoint drilling habit transfers directly from the earlier games.

The route memory is the skill that the game's length demands. The long levels chain dozens of jumps into a single run, and the players who memorized the route, knowing the jump arcs and the platform timings before the run started, executed the chains smoothly, while the players who improvised the routes made the decisions under the time pressure that the long levels impose. Our testing found that the memorized runs cleared the long levels with a fraction of the deaths of the improvised runs.

The stamina management is the game's hidden challenge. The precision platforming demands the full attention, and the attention fades over the long sessions, which means the players who played past the focus limit died on the simple jumps that the fresh players cleared easily, and our analysis shows that the act-by-act practice, with the rests between the acts, kept the attention fresh for the gauntlets that the marathon sessions could not hold.

The mechanic continuity is the trap for the returning players. The earlier games' habits, the jump arcs and the slide timings, carry into Vex 5 but with the level-design variations that punish the assumption, and our testing found that the players who treated the first act as a recalibration, re-measuring the jumps against the new level geometry, adapted faster than the players who assumed the old measurements still held.

The act themes are visual, but the difficulty tiers they mark are real, and the players who read the tier shifts adjusted their practice accordingly. The theme change between the acts signals the new hazard mix, and our analysis shows that the players who scouted the first level of each new act slowly, learning the act's new combinations before the speed runs, avoided the death streaks that the theme-blind players suffered, since the new act always introduces at least one new way to die.

The death-replay habit matters more in the long levels, where a single death can erase minutes of progress. Our testing found that the players who watched each death replay, identifying whether the mistake was the jump distance, the platform timing, or the route choice, fixed the error before the next attempt, while the players who restarted instantly repeated the same death at the same section, and the replay discipline converted the long-level frustration into the measured improvement.

The practice levels and the secret areas offer the low-stakes drilling that the hard levels need. The game hides the optional areas that reuse the mechanics without the gauntlet pressure, and our analysis shows that the players who mined the optional areas for the move practice, particularly the wall-jump rhythm and the slide timing, built the fluency that the main levels demanded, and the optional practice translated directly into the fewer deaths on the mandatory gauntlets.

3. Step-by-Step Level Map

The level loop below is the sequence we followed during the testing that produced our fastest full-game clears.

Process Map
  1. 1Refresh the moves
  2. 2Scout the act's first level
  3. 3Memorize the long route
  4. 4Run the memorized route
  5. 5Drill at the checkpoints
  6. 6Rest between the acts

Follow this numbered routine for the game's long levels:

  1. Refresh the moves before each act — drill the jumps, the slides, and the wall-jumps in the act's early levels until the vocabulary is fluent.
  2. Scout the act's new mix — walk the first level of each act slowly and learn the new hazard combinations before the fast runs.
  3. Memorize the long routes — walk each long level until the jump arcs and the platform timings are known, then run the memorized route.
  4. Drill the hard sections at the checkpoints — repeat each difficult piece until it is clean, then chain the pieces into the full run.
  5. Rest between the acts — stop at the act boundaries and return with the fresh attention that the gauntlets demand.

The act-by-act practice is the single most important habit for the full-game clear. Our testing found that the players who treated each act as a chapter, mastering its gauntlet and resting before the next, cleared the game with a fraction of the deaths of the players who marathon-rushed the whole list in one tilted session.

The route memorization is the skill that the final gauntlets reward. The late levels chain the full vocabulary into the long sequences, and our analysis shows that the players who walked the routes until the arcs were known, then ran the memorized paths, cleared the gauntlets, while the players who improvised the routes died at the transitions that the memorized runners crossed without thinking.

The jump-arc calibration is the first skill to re-check in the biggest entry, since the level geometry varies more than the earlier games. The stickman's jump distance depends on the run speed and the hold time, and our testing found that the players who practiced the short-hop and the full-jump arcs against the new platforms, measuring the distances the new level design demanded, landed the narrow ledges consistently, while the players who carried the old measurements overshot the new platforms that the wider level variety introduced.

The momentum management carries more weight in the long levels, where the speed bleeds and the precision compounds. The stickman carries the run speed into the jumps, and the speed that clears the wide gaps overshoots the narrow landings, and our analysis shows that the players who bled the speed before the tight sections, tapping back or jumping from the controlled approach, landed the platforms that the full-speed players flew past, and the controlled approaches are what the long levels' mixed geometry demands.

4. Expert Troubleshooting and Optimization

Vex 5 progress stalls in predictable ways, and each fix below is one we confirmed through our own retry tracking.

  • The early acts are easy but the late acts crush the runs — the skills are not being carried forward; master each act's gauntlet before advancing and refresh the weak moves between the acts.
  • The long levels end in the same section — the route is not memorized; walk the level until the arcs are known, then run the memorized path instead of improvising.
  • The deaths spike after thirty minutes of play — the focus is fading; practice act by act with the rests between, and stop when the attention drops.
  • The moves feel off in the new acts — the recalibration is missing; scout the first level of each act slowly and re-measure the jumps against the new geometry.
  • The final gauntlets feel impossible — the pieces are not drilled; mine each section at the checkpoint until it is clean, then chain the pieces into the full run.

The total deaths per act is the game's real scoreboard. Our testing found that the players who tracked the deaths per act, rather than the total play time, saw which acts demanded the extra practice, and the tracking directed the drilling to the weak acts while the play time only measured the effort.

A player who refreshes the moves, memorizes the routes, and rests between the acts will watch the average fall, and that falling average, tracked across the full level list of the series' biggest entry, is what truly mastering the biggest Vex levels in the whole of the series means at the end.
