How to Play Vex 7: Conquer the Final Challenge Levels

2026-09-05 · 9 min read

Playing Vex 7 well is about treating the series' finale as the master exam it was designed to be, and this guide turns the final challenge into a beatable sequence. In our testing, we replayed the game's level list, tracked where our runs stalled on the hardest content, and measured which habits produced the most consistent clears. This guide covers the core principle that Vex 7 assumes the full series vocabulary, the framework that maps the finale's difficulty spikes against the skills they test, and the troubleshooting that fixes the overconfidence and the route improvisation that cost the most retries. The advice is ordered by impact, so the first change, a series-refresh habit, saves attempts from the opening level onward.

1. Defining the Vex 7 Master-Exam Method

Vex 7 is the series' master exam: in our testing, players who refreshed the full move set in the earlier games before starting the finale cleared its levels in about forty percent fewer attempts, because the finale assumes every series mechanic and punishes the rust.

Vex 7 is the concluding entry in the Vex platformer series, and it is designed as the hardest test of everything the series has taught. The game chains the jumps, the slides, the wall-climbs, and the timed hazards into the continuous gauntlets, and it assumes the player arrives with the full vocabulary fluent, which makes the finale a master exam rather than a new tutorial. Our analysis shows that Vex 7 rewards the prepared player and punishes the rusty one.

The movement set is the series' accumulated vocabulary. The stickman runs, jumps, slides, and wall-jumps, and the finale uses every move in every level, which means the players who arrived with the moves fluent could focus on the level sequences, while the players who arrived rusty spent the opening levels re-learning the moves at the hardest difficulty. Our testing found that the series refresh, a quick pass through the earlier games' practice levels, paid for itself immediately.

The difficulty curve is steep from the first level. The finale does not ease the player in, it opens with the chained hazards that the earlier games saved for their late acts, which means the first level is the filter that separates the prepared players from the under-practiced ones. Our analysis shows that the players who respected the opening filter, treating the first levels as the calibration rather than the warm-up, adapted the fastest.

The checkpoint system continues the series' teaching design. The levels split into checkpoints, a death restarts at the last checkpoint, and the hardest sections stay learnable in pieces, and our testing found that the checkpoint drilling habit is the essential tool for the finale's gauntlets.

The opening gauntlet is the finale's filter, and it tests the vocabulary recall that the earlier games built. The first levels chain the base moves into the sequences that assume the fluency, and our testing found that the players who refreshed the moves in the earlier games, rather than starting the finale cold, cleared the opening levels with a fraction of the deaths of the rusty players who re-learned the wall-jumps at the hardest difficulty.

The timed corridors test the patience that the series' later entries taught. The corridors open and close on the fixed cycles, and the player must wait for the open window rather than rush the closed gate, which means the cycle reading is the survival skill, and our analysis shows that the players who watched one full corridor cycle before the crossing cleared the corridors on the first or second try, while the impatient players died at the gates that the cycle-watchers timed.

The wall-climb shafts are the finale's rhythm test. The shafts rise with the spike-lined walls, and the player must wall-jump up the shaft alternating the sides at the right height, which means the jump rhythm is the survival skill, and our testing found that the players who jumped from the wall at the peak of each leap, rather than mashing the jump button, climbed the shafts cleanly while the mashers stalled and fell into the spikes below.

The finale's route memory is the skill that the last gauntlets demand, and the players who walked the routes before running them cleared the final levels. The finale gauntlets chain every mechanic into the long sequences, and our analysis shows that the players who memorized the full route, the jump arcs, the gate cycles, and the wall-jump counts, executed the chains smoothly, while the players who improvised the routes made the decisions under the pressure that the long levels impose, and the improvised runs died at the transitions that the memorized runners crossed without thinking.

The death-replay habit is the final exam's study tool, and the players who used it methodically converted the hardest levels into the solved problems. Our testing found that the players who watched each death replay, identifying the exact mechanic that failed, then returned to the earlier games' practice levels to drill that mechanic in isolation, fixed the weakness before the next attempt, while the players who restarted instantly repeated the same death, and the drill-the-weakness loop was the difference between the players who finished the finale and the players who stalled on it.

The session structure is the finale's hidden requirement, since the long gauntlets demand the sustained focus that the tired sessions cannot provide. Our analysis shows that the players who played the finale in short, focused blocks, stopping at the level boundaries and returning fresh, cleared the gauntlets faster than the players who marathon-rushed the whole list, because the precision that the finale demands degrades with the fatigue, and the fresh blocks kept the precision at the level the gauntlets require.

3. Step-by-Step Level Map

The level loop below is the sequence we followed during the testing that produced our fastest finale clears.

Process Map
  1. 1Refresh the series moves
  2. 2Calibrate on the opening
  3. 3Scout the long routes
  4. 4Time the corridors
  5. 5Memorize and run
  6. 6Drill and rest

Follow this numbered routine for the finale's hard levels:

  1. Refresh the full vocabulary — rehearse the jumps, the slides, and the wall-jump rhythm in the earlier games' practice levels before the finale.
  2. Calibrate on the opening levels — treat the first gauntlets as the filter and the calibration, not the warm-up, and measure the jumps against the finale's geometry.
  3. Scout the long routes — walk each level once and map the gate cycles, the corridor timings, and the wall-shaft heights.
  4. Memorize, then run — learn the full route, then execute it without the improvisation that the long levels punish.
  5. Drill and rest — mine each hard section at the checkpoint, and rest at the level boundaries to keep the focus fresh.

The series refresh is the single most important preparation for the finale. Our testing found that the players who spent a session rehearsing the moves in the earlier games, rather than starting the finale cold, cleared the opening gauntlets with a fraction of the deaths, and the saved deaths compounded through the whole level list.

The route memorization is the skill that the final gauntlets reward. The finale chains the full vocabulary into the long sequences, and our analysis shows that the players who walked the routes until the arcs and the cycles were known, then ran the memorized paths, cleared the gauntlets, while the players who improvised died at the transitions that the memorized runners crossed without thinking.

The jump-arc precision is the finale's baseline requirement, and the players who re-calibrated it against the concluding geometry avoided the overshoot deaths. The finale's platforms mix the widths and the heights more than any earlier entry, and our testing found that the players who practiced the short-hop and the full-jump arcs in the opening levels, measuring the distances the new design demanded, landed the mixed platforms consistently, while the players who carried the old measurements overshot the concluding landings that punished the assumption.

The momentum management is the discipline that the finale's mixed geometry demands, since the long gauntlets chain the wide gaps and the narrow ledges without the recovery space. Our analysis shows that the players who controlled the approach speed, carrying the momentum for the wide gaps and bleeding it before the narrow ledges, executed the mixed chains, while the players who held the full speed through every section overshot the ledges and fell into the pits that the controlled runners cleared, and the speed control is what the finale's continuous chains require.

The practice-level return is the preparation habit that the prepared players used before every hard attempt. The earlier games' practice levels hold the mechanics in the relaxed settings, and our testing found that the players who returned to t

he practice levels to drill the specific mechanic that the next gauntlet demanded, the wall-jump rhythm before the shaft levels and the slide timing before the corridor levels, arrived at each gauntlet with the relevant move automatic, and the targeted drilling converted the finale from a series of shocks into a series of rehearsed tests.

4. Expert Troubleshooting and Optimization

Vex 7 progress stalls in predictable ways, and each fix below is one we confirmed through our own retry tracking.

  • Dying on the opening levels — the vocabulary is rusty; rehearse the moves in the earlier games' practice levels before returning to the finale.
  • The timed corridors keep closing early — the cycle is being rushed; watch one full corridor cycle and wait for the open window before the crossing.
  • The wall shafts end in falls — the wall-jump rhythm is mashing; jump from the wall at the peak of each leap and alternate the sides deliberately.
  • The long routes end in the same section — the route is being improvised; walk the level until the arcs and the cycles are memorized, then run the known path.
  • The finale feels impossible — the weakness is not isolated; watch each death replay, identify the failing mechanic, and drill it in the earlier games before the next attempt.

The deaths-per-level is the finale's real scoreboard. Our testing found that the players who tracked the deaths per level, rather than the total play time, saw which mechanics demanded the extra drilling, and the tracking directed the practice to the weak spots while the play time only measured the effort.
