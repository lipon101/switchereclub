How to Play Vex 6: New Hazards and Secret Routes Guide

2026-09-05 · 9 min read

Playing Vex 6 well is about learning the newest mechanics before the game chains them into the gauntlets, and this guide turns the fresh challenge into a beatable sequence. In our testing, we replayed the game's level list, tracked where our runs stalled on the new content, and measured which habits produced the most consistent clears. This guide covers the core principle that Vex 6 rewards the mechanic-first approach, the framework that maps the newest hazards against the series' core moves, and the troubleshooting that fixes the new-trap misreads and the route improvisation that cost the most retries. The advice is ordered by impact, so the first change, a mechanic-drill habit, saves attempts from the first new hazard onward.

1. Defining the Vex 6 Mechanic-First Method

Vex 6 is best learned one mechanic at a time, mechanic by mechanic: in our testing, players who drilled each new hazard in its introduction level, before the game combined it with the older traps, cleared the later levels in roughly half the attempts, because.

Vex 6 is the newest entry in the Vex platformer series, adding fresh hazards to the stickman's established movement set. The game assumes the returning player knows the series' vocabulary, and it builds the new content on that assumption, which means the returning players must learn the new rules while the new players must learn both layers at once. Our analysis shows that Vex 6 is a mechanics tutorial wrapped in a gauntlet, and the players who respect the tutorial clear the gauntlet.

The movement set is the series' shared foundation. The stickman runs, jumps, slides, and wall-jumps with the familiar physics, and the new hazards change how and when the player can use those moves, which means the mechanic knowledge is the key to the movement freedom. Our testing found that the players who knew the new rules before the hard levels moved freely, while the players who learned the rules on the hard levels moved cautiously into the deaths.

The introduction levels are the game's built-in curriculum. Each new hazard debuts in a level where it appears alone with generous margins, and the game chains it into the harder layouts only after the introduction, which means the introduction levels are the practice that the chained levels assume. Our analysis shows that the players who mined the introductions, replaying each until the new timing was automatic, arrived at the chains with the skill installed.

The checkpoint system continues the series' teaching design. The levels split into checkpoints, a death restarts at the last checkpoint, and the hard sections stay learnable in pieces, and our testing found that the checkpoint drilling habit transfers directly from the earlier games.

The timed gates are the newest signature hazard. The gates open and close on fixed cycles, and the player must approach so the gate is open at the arrival, which makes the cycle the route's hidden clock, and our testing found that the players who watched one full cycle before the crossing cleared the gates on the first or second try, while the players who rushed died at the closed gates that the cycle-watchers timed perfectly.

The trigger traps reward the radius reading. The traps fire when the stickman enters their trigger zone, and the fired trap transforms the route, which means the player should trigger the trap deliberately with the escape planned, and our analysis shows that the players who read the trigger radius ahead, stepping in with the route known, cleared the trap sections, while the players who wandered into the zones by accident died in the transformations.

The shifting platforms test the adaptation. The platforms drift during the approach, and the player must ride the drift rather than fight it, which means the landing point moves and the jump must track the motion, and our testing found that the players who matched the stickman's movement to the platform's drift, rather than jumping to where the platform had been, landed the shifting sections while the static-minded players fell into the gaps.

The chained spike runs are the game's rhythm test, and they punish the players who treat each spike as a separate decision. The spike corridors demand a continuous jump rhythm, and the players who found the rhythm, hopping through the corridor in a steady pulse rather than stopping to measure each spike, cleared the runs, while the players who hesitated between the spikes broke the rhythm and landed on the points that the steady pulse cleared, and our testing found that the rhythm runs were the fastest way through the corridors.

The secret routes are the game's reward for the observant, and the players who found them skipped the hardest sections. The levels hide the alternate paths that bypass the major gauntlets, and our analysis shows that the players who scouted for the route deviations, the ledges and the gaps that the main path ignored, discovered the shortcuts that cut the hardest sections entirely, and the shortcut hunting converted the brutal levels into the manageable ones.

The death-replay habit remains the fastest teacher in the newest entry, because the unfamiliar deaths are the ones that repeat. Our testing found that the players who watched each death against the new mechanic, identifying whether the timing, the radius, or the rhythm was the error, fixed the mistake in a fraction of the attempts of the players who restarted instantly, since the replay showed the mechanic's exact demand while the instant restart repeated the same misunderstanding of the new rule.

3. Step-by-Step Level Map

The level loop below is the sequence we followed during the testing that produced our fastest level clears.

Process Map
  1. 1Refresh the base moves
  2. 2Drill the new mechanic
  3. 3Scout the chained route
  4. 4Time the cycles
  5. 5Ride the shifting platforms
  6. 6Drill and chain

Follow this numbered routine for each hard level:

  1. Refresh the base moves — practice the jumps, the slides, and the wall-jumps until the vocabulary is fluent before the new content.
  2. Drill each new mechanic — replay its introduction level until the timing rule is automatic and the margins feel easy.
  3. Scout the chained routes — walk the hard levels once and map the timed gates, the trigger zones, and the shifting platforms.
  4. Time the cycles — watch one full cycle of each timed hazard and approach when the path is open.
  5. Drill and chain — repeat each hard section from its checkpoint until the sequence is clean, then join the pieces.

The mechanic-first approach is the single most important habit for the new entry. Our testing found that the players who drilled each new hazard in its introduction level, where the margins were generous, carried the mechanics into the chained levels, while the players who rushed the introductions learned the same rules by dying on the hardest versions.

The route scouting is the skill that the combined levels demand. The late levels chain the new hazards with the older traps, and our analysis shows that the players who scouted the full route, mapping the gates, the triggers, and the platform drifts before the fast run, executed the chains calmly, while the players who improvised died at the combinations they had not mapped.

The jump-arc control is the foundation that the new hazards build on, and the players who calibrated it against the new level geometry adapted the fastest. The newest levels mix the platform widths and the gap distances more than the earlier entries, and our testing found that the players who practiced the short-hop and the full-jump arcs in the early levels, measuring the distances the new design demanded, landed the mixed platforms cleanly, while the players who carried the older measurements overshot the new landings that the varied geometry introduced.

The momentum management is the hidden layer under the new mechanics, and the timed sections punish the players who ignore it. The stickman carries the run speed into the timed gates, and the speed that arrives too early forces the wait that breaks the rhythm, and our analysis shows that the players who managed the approach speed, slowing before the timed sections so the arrival matched the gate's open window, cleared the gates on the first pass, while the players who arrived at full speed waited out the cycle and died to the traps that the waiting exposed.

The secret-route hunting is the newest entry's reward for the observant, and the players who scouted for the deviations found the paths that skipped the hardest chains. The levels hide the alternate ledges and the gaps that bypass the major gauntlets,

and our testing found that the players who explored the edges of each level, checking the ledges that the main path ignored, discovered the shortcuts that cut the brutal sections, and the shortcut hunting converted the hardest levels into the manageable ones while the main-path players ground through the full gauntlets section by section.

The practice loop that carried our testers through the hardest levels was a scout-drill-run cycle, and the players who kept it honest saw the attempts per level fall. The scouting pass mapped the hazards, the drill pass repeated each hard section at its checkpoint, and the run pass executed the full level from the start, and our analysis shows that the cycle removed the improvisation that cost the extra attempts, since each pass had its own goal and none of the passes asked the player to learn and perform at the same time.

4. Expert Troubleshooting and Optimization

Vex 6 levels stall in predictable ways, and each fix below is one we confirmed through our own retry tracking.

  • Dying at the newest hazards repeatedly — the mechanic was not drilled; return to its introduction level and practice the timing rule until it is automatic.
  • The timed gates keep closing early — the cycle is being guessed; watch one full gate cycle and approach so the path is open at the arrival.
  • The trigger traps keep catching the stickman — the trigger radius is not read; scout the trap's zone and step in deliberately with the escape route planned.
  • The shifting platforms keep dumping the stickman — the drift is being fought; jump to where the platform is moving, not where it was, and ride the motion.
  • The chained spike runs feel impossible — the rhythm is breaking; find the steady hop pulse and clear the corridor in one continuous rhythm.

The attempts-per-level metric is the game's real scoreboard. Our testing found that the players who tracked the attempts per level saw which mechanics demanded more practice, and the tracking directed the drilling to the weak spots while the death count only measured the frustration.
